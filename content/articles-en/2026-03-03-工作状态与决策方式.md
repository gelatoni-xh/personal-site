# Work and Decision-Making in an HR Master Data Team

## 1. The Shape of the Work

During my time on the HR master data system, my work was split roughly in half: QA support and problem diagnosis on one side, and coding and system changes on the other.

The system served as both a data platform and a business platform. It integrated with more than 1,000 upstream systems, received frequent calls around the clock, and had strong requirements for stability and data correctness. The job therefore resembled maintaining critical infrastructure more than building isolated features.

## 2. Where Daily Work Came From

### Ongoing Support for Upstream Systems

This included investigating production issues with other teams, explaining business concepts and API usage, and supporting changes in calling systems. Requests were often urgent and customer-facing, could directly affect stability, and sometimes involved overnight on-call work. I treated them as the first priority because the calling systems were our customers.

### Company-Level Projects

Examples included organizational separation, changes to level structures and organization models, and data-model upgrades. These initiatives affected a wide area, required careful design and compatibility with historical callers, and were expensive to verify. Business needs usually drove them, so they often took priority over internal technical improvements.

### Technical Governance

This covered technical debt, API consolidation, architectural decoupling, and data-model improvements. Such work often took a long time. Much of the effort was in early design and later coordination, so visible progress could come in stages. It was easiest to advance when business delivery was less pressing.

## 3. How I Set Priorities

When these demands competed, my usual order was:

1. Stability and production problems affecting upstream systems.
2. Company-level business projects.
3. Technical governance work.

The principle was to protect stability before pursuing optimization, and to serve calling teams before undertaking self-directed improvements.

For a proposed model change, I would ask whether it affected compatibility for existing callers, whether existing APIs could already provide the needed behavior, whether a data-model change was necessary, and whether the risks were manageable. When risk was too high, I favored a conservative design.

## 4. My Technical Preferences

### Stability First

I tried to avoid disruptive changes, preserve backward compatibility, limit the affected area, and put effort into verification and tests.

### Readability Over Cleverness

In an infrastructure-like system, maintainable code mattered more than clever techniques, clear structure more than individual style, and a simple design more than unnecessary abstraction. The person maintaining the code later might not be its author.

## 5. Risk and Coordination

When a technical change was disputed, I formed my own view, weighed cost and benefit, and discussed the risks and effort with my manager. Once the organization made a decision, I supported its execution. Afterwards I reviewed actual costs, whether the anticipated risks occurred, and whether the decision process could be improved. The aim was to improve long-term decisions through evidence and cooperation.

## 6. Learning a Large System

The HR master data system had a large codebase, many business domains, a one-to-two-year learning curve, and tightly coupled historical structures. I focused on understanding the business domains, dependencies of calling systems, and the broader effect of a change while keeping an independent view of architecture problems. Pursuing technical depth alone would not have been enough for this work.

## 7. Working Style

My approach emphasized stability, early risk assessment, structured reasoning, organizational coordination, conservative compatibility decisions, and business understanding. It suited a long-lived platform with many dependent systems and high stability requirements.

## 8. What This Environment Offered and Limited

This working style fits platform systems with many callers and strict reliability needs. It also leaves limited room for architectural experimentation, gives coding a smaller share of the day, and can make progress less visible. The pace of technical growth can depend heavily on organizational priorities. Those trade-offs informed how I thought about later career choices.
