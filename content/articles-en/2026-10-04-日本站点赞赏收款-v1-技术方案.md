# Japanese-Site Support Payments: V1 Technical Plan

## 1. Goal

This version addressed one question: how to give Japanese visitors a support or tip path that could go live quickly. It did not aim for complete coverage of Japanese payment methods, local Japanese merchant acquiring, one consistent experience for Chinese users, or strict reconciliation of confirmed payments.

The priorities were to let Japanese users support the site, test whether real support intent existed, and establish pages and events that could later accommodate Ko-fi.

## 2. Constraints

At the time of this plan, the operator had mainland Chinese personal identity documents but no Japanese legal entity, Japanese identity documents, or Japanese bank account. The first phase therefore did not use Stripe Japan or PayPay merchant acquiring. PayPal was the proposed first option, with Ko-fi reserved for a second phase. A Google Ads landing page and conversion-related tracking belonged in the same design.

The central idea was a Japanese-language support entry point, a cross-border payment method available to the operator, and site-side visibility into clicks and campaign performance. It was not a local acquiring integration.

## 3. Core Decisions

### Payment Entry

V1 proposed PayPal as the only support entry. It avoided dependence on Japanese entity documents, could be launched relatively quickly, was a recognizable payment brand for Japanese visitors, and could later be supplemented by Ko-fi. These were selection reasons, not proof that the account and payment route had been approved or tested.

### Main Page

Rather than placing an unexplained PayPal button around the site, the proposal created `/ja/support`. A multilingual site might generalize it to `/[locale]/support`, but V1 targeted Japanese traffic. A dedicated page could explain the project and intended use of funds, build trust, collect events, and later change payment providers without rebuilding the page structure.

### Advertising Page

`/ja/support/lp` was a proposed short, conversion-focused landing page. Compared with the regular support page, it would have less content, a tighter value proposition and CTA, and a format suitable for ad A/B tests.

## 4. Page Content

The regular page would contain a one-sentence site description, why support helps, where funds go, how support works, a PayPal button, a note about possible future options, and a short FAQ or trust explanation.

The ad page would use a concise hero, one value statement, a short explanation of why support matters, one primary CTA, and limited trust information. The payment entry should be visible without requiring a long scroll.

## 5. Payment Flow

The first phase proposed a redirect to `PayPal.me` or another PayPal receiving page, avoiding a custom on-site checkout. That reduced development effort, compliance complexity, and the scope of payment-failure diagnosis.

The page could provide fixed-amount shortcuts and a custom amount. If PayPal's receiving page could not support both easily, a single default entry was acceptable. For Japanese visitors, amounts should preferably be displayed in JPY even if cross-border conversion occurred later on PayPal's side.

## 6. Events and Ad Attribution

A button alone would not provide enough evidence. V1 should record support-page views, support-button clicks, landing-page views, and landing-page CTA clicks. Proposed event names were `support_page_view`, `support_cta_click`, `support_lp_view`, and `support_lp_cta_click`, with `locale`, `page_type`, `source`, `campaign`, and `content` fields.

The landing page should retain common UTM parameters: `utm_source`, `utm_medium`, `utm_campaign`, and `utm_content`. Those fields would make it possible to compare traffic and click behavior across campaigns and entry pages.

## 7. Search and Language

The regular `/ja/support` page could be indexed. Whether `/ja/support/lp` should be indexed depended on the advertising strategy; `noindex` might be appropriate for an experimental ad page. Japanese copy needed to read naturally, with a restrained tone, a clear title, and a natural CTA rather than a literal Chinese-to-Japanese translation.

## 8. Risks

**PayPal trust:** Some Japanese visitors may not trust or use PayPal enough for strong conversion. The page should explain the site's background and use of funds, and Ko-fi could be considered later.

**Incomplete payment feedback:** V1 could reliably observe a click to PayPal but might not receive a confirmed-payment callback. That was acceptable for an initial interest test, but click data must not be reported as completed donations.

**Cross-border personal-account limits:** Before launch, the operator needed to verify that a personal PayPal account could receive the intended payments, that Japanese visitors could open the receiving page consistently, and that platform risk controls would not block the flow.

## 9. Proposed Sequence

1. Prepare and verify the PayPal receiving entry.
2. Publish `/ja/support`.
3. Publish `/ja/support/lp`.
4. Add basic event tracking and UTM handling.
5. Run a small ad test.
6. Revise copy and CTA from click and engagement data.

## 10. Conclusion

The V1 proposal favored a PayPal-first redirect, a regular Japanese support page, an ad-specific page, and basic tracking. Its value would be a low-cost test of whether visitors wanted to support the content, while leaving space for Ko-fi or other methods later. It was a plan to test intent, not evidence that payment conversion had already been achieved.
